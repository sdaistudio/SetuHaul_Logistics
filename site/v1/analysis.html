1. What data we have

The SQL seed rebuilds a database identical to the supplied .db (18 tables, 4 views, every row equal; integrity check OK; no foreign-key violations). One small, deliberately messy operational day: mostly Jaipur, with a single Gurugram shipment.

How a shipment moves through the data

shipment → driver + vehicle → destination facility → docks → appointment slots → appointment (one current, with history). In transit: eta_updates. At the gate: facility_checkins. In chat: chat_threads → chat_messages → driver_exceptions. To the warehouse: operational_messages.

Shipment mix

Views shipped with the DB: v_latest_eta, v_slot_availability, v_inbound_operational_state, v_current_facility_queue. Constraints: one active booking per slot, one current booking per shipment.

Driver-declared ETA vs actual gate-in

Every truck that later checked in had declared its ETA 25–55 minutes ahead, and all four were within 5 minutes. Short-horizon driver declarations look reliable; the uncertain ones are long-horizon and "around / late by" messages.

Planning buffer per confidence level (assumed arrival spread; compare with rules.yaml)

Buffer = z × σ for an 80% / 90% chance the truck is on the dock by the planned start. σ values are assumptions until real arrivals are logged (the four observed HIGH errors had σ ≈ 2 min). Current MEDIUM 15 and LOW 30 match the 80% level; HIGH 0 is fine at short horizons, too optimistic for declarations made 2+ hours ahead.

4. Capacity: the problem is timing, not total supply

What the numbers say

The 11:00 hour is the crunch: more standard trucks than standard slot starts, while 31 standard slots after 14:00 sit empty. The solver's real job is to spread the late-morning wave across the empty afternoon with the least priority-weighted waiting, not to find capacity that doesn't exist.

5. Unload time and how delays cascade on a dock

Monte Carlo: delay of the 8th truck on one dock, back-to-back 60-min slots

20,000 runs per point. Trucks arrive on time on average (σ 10 min). Once mean unload reaches the slot length (utilisation 1.0) a dock cannot recover: 70–75-minute unloads in 60-minute slots push the 8th truck 70–110 minutes late whatever the variability. 4 of the 20 bookings in the data already spill (by 13.8 min on average).

8. Data quality (columns with missing values)

Expected gaps (not yet happened: unload end, gate out, closed_at) are fine. Problem gaps: replaced_appointment_id is empty for every row even though the guide says APT1012A/APT1016A were replaced (history can't be followed); latest_acceptable_ts is filled in 1 of 10 exceptions, so driver deadlines are mostly lost; external_message_id is missing on 40% of messages, which weakens dedupe; the Gurugram night-shift email is missing.

10. Caveats

  • Tiny sample. 21 shipments, 12 ETA updates, 12 driver messages, 4 observed arrivals, 1 completed unload. Significance tests are shown for completeness; none should drive policy on their own. The weight-vs-slip correlation (ρ 0.50, p 0.02) is likely confounded by which trucks are still in transit.
  • Seeded, not observed. The day was built to exercise edge cases, so edge cases are over-represented compared with a normal day.
  • Mixed clock. The snapshot holds events from 07:10 to 16:30; "now" was fixed at 09:30 for yard waits.
  • Assumptions in the simulations (arrival σ by confidence, unload CV) are labelled and editable in analyze.py; replace them with fitted values after 2–4 weeks of real data.
  • Language. All 12 seeded driver messages are English; the Hindi/Hinglish coverage comes from our own test sets, not from this data.
